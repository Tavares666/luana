\begin{abstract}
 \textbf{Research Context:}
 \textit{Digital transformation has expanded the role of municipal electronic government (e-Gov) in delivering public services, transparency, participation, communication, and citizen interaction. However, the existence of digital channels alone does not indicate the maturity of a local digital government ecosystem.}
 \textbf{Scientific and/or Practical Problem:}  
 \textit{Municipalities lack a unified, evidence-based, and comparable instrument for assessing e-Gov digital maturity across multiple dimensions, combining institutional, technological, service, accessibility, participation, and citizen-oriented aspects.}
 \textbf{Proposed Solution and/or Analysis:}  
 \textit{This study proposes a multidimensional Framework for Evaluating e-Gov Digital Maturity, composed of ten dimensions: Digital Presence; Ombudsman Services; Organizational Structure; Transparency; Communication Channels; Participation; Service Digitalization; Digital Accessibility; Citizen Satisfaction; and Interoperability, Open Data, and Security. The framework produces a composite Municipal Digital Maturity Index and classifies municipalities into five maturity levels.}
 \textbf{Related IS Theory: }  
 \textit{The framework is grounded in e-Gov maturity and digital government assessment approaches, particularly the UN e-Government Survey logic for online service and e-participation maturity, complemented by Brazilian public-sector transparency, accessibility, and service regulations.}
 \textbf{Research Method:} 
 \textit{The study adopts a multi-method evaluation protocol combining structured checklists, secondary public data, automated web collection, accessibility auditing, and natural language processing. The instrument is piloted across representative municipalities, refined, and structured for application to the 142 municipalities of Mato Grosso.}
 \textbf{Summary of Results:} 
 \textit{The current stage defines the framework's dimensions, indicators, data collection strategies, weighting structure, and a five-level maturity scale, supported by a preliminary pilot demonstration that confirms indicator clarity and establishes the foundation for large-scale empirical application.}
 \textbf{Contributions and Impact to IS area:} 
 \textit{The study contributes a replicable and scalable instrument for evaluating municipal digital government maturity and supports comparative analysis, longitudinal monitoring, and evidence-based improvement of public-sector Information Systems.}
 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The digital transformation of public administrations has broadened the ways in which services are delivered and in which governments and citizens interact. Despite the expansion of portals, transactional platforms, and participation channels, their mere availability does not demonstrate that services are accessible, integrated, responsive, or effectively used. The \textit{UN E-Government Survey 2024} highlights persistent gaps between the development of national and local online services, assessing municipal service provision through the \textit{Local Online Services Index} (LOSI) \cite{un2024egovernment}. The \textit{GovTech Maturity Index 2025}, in turn, recognizes four complementary areas (core systems, digital public services, engagement, and enablers), but adopts national economies as its main unit of analysis \cite{worldbank2025gtmi}.

Comparison at the municipal level requires greater granularity. Local governments within the same state may share legal obligations yet differ in administrative capacity, infrastructure, interaction channels, service coverage, and mechanisms for evaluation by users. In the recent literature, the review by \cite{david2023understanding} identifies the interdependence among people, processes, and technology in municipal digital adoption, whereas \cite{toleikiene2025assessing} and \cite{mkhitaryan2026digital} provide empirical evidence that institutional and service dimensions must be examined at the local scale. In Brazil, \cite{vieira2026modelos} identify the diversity of maturity models and the absence of a consolidated instrument for the public sector as a whole.

The state of Mato Grosso comprises 142 municipalities, Boa Esperança do Norte being the most recently created \cite{ibge2025municipios,mt2025ppa}. For a state-level analysis, national indicators of transparency, accessibility, and digital government must be translated into verifiable criteria on municipal government websites, linked portals, public documents, and, when available, administrative records. The 2026 update of the National Digital Government Strategy (\textit{Estratégia Nacional de Governo Digital}, ENGD) reinforces intergovernmental coordination, inclusion, secure data sharing, and participation as components of government modernization \cite{brasil2026portaria5395}.

In this context, the research question is: how can a multidimensional, editable, and replicable instrument be built and operationalized to assess the digital maturity of municipal government without reducing the analysis to the existence of a portal or to the publication of data? The general objective is to propose a framework and a Municipal Digital Maturity Index (\textit{Índice de Maturidade Digital Municipal}, IMDM) applicable to the 142 municipalities of Mato Grosso. Specifically, the study aims to define dimensions and indicators, identify sources of evidence, establish normalization and aggregation rules, conduct a pilot study, and specify a validation protocol that enables spatial comparisons and reapplications over time. 

The intended contribution is methodological and instrumental: to articulate dimensions already discussed in recent studies with verifiable indicators at the Brazilian municipal scale. The research is presented at the design and development stage of the artifact; a preliminary demonstration was conducted in three municipalities, while the full application and validation of the index remain planned for subsequent stages.


\section{Background Material}

This section presents the conceptual and regulatory foundations that guide the construction of the framework. It first discusses the concept of digital maturity in the public sector, stage models, and the main international assessment indices, as well as the methodological decisions involved in building composite indices. It then examines the Brazilian context, addressing the legal frameworks that establish transparency, citizen service, and digital government obligations for municipalities, and the implications of these obligations for the assessment of municipal portals, including digital accessibility and citizen satisfaction.

\subsection{Digital Government and Maturity Models}

Digital maturity in the public sector is not limited to the availability of web pages. It involves the capacity to organize processes, provide services, enable citizen participation, and integrate information systems. In a stage model, \cite{layneLee2001} describe a trajectory that begins with the cataloging of information, advances to electronic transactions, and reaches forms of vertical and horizontal integration.

This perspective provides a reference for assessing service digitalization, but it does not fully cover aspects such as accessibility, institutional structure, and the responsiveness of digital channels. The \textit{UN E-Government Survey 2024} examines e-government development at the national scale and also presents the \textit{Local Online Services Index} (LOSI), intended for the analysis of city portals. The 2024 edition points to persistent gaps between the development of national and local online services \cite{un2024egovernment}. 

In turn, the 2025 \textit{GovTech Maturity Index} (GTMI) organizes the assessment into four areas: core systems and shared digital infrastructure, online public service delivery, digital engagement, and enablers \cite{worldbank2025gtmi}. These instruments demonstrate the usefulness of multidimensional approaches, although their units of analysis, coverage, and procedures do not match the requirements of a census-based assessment of the municipalities of Mato Grosso.

The construction of a composite index requires explicit decisions regarding indicator definition, the treatment of missing data, normalization, weighting, and aggregation. The methodological handbook of the OECD and the European Commission's Joint Research Centre recommends documenting these choices and examining the robustness of the results under different assumptions \cite{oecd2008}. In the present work, the ten dimensions are components of the phenomenon being assessed. Therefore, inter-rater agreement in the use of the indicators and the justification of the proposed weights are distinct steps in the validation of the Municipal Digital Maturity Index (IMDM).

\subsection{Assessment of Municipal Portals in the Brazilian Context}

Municipal assessment must simultaneously consider the delivery of digital services and the obligations of transparency and citizen service. Law No. 12,527/2011 regulates access to public information; Law No. 13,460/2017 provides for the participation, protection, and defense of the rights of public service users; and Law No. 14,129/2021 establishes principles and instruments for Digital Government and for increasing public efficiency \cite{brasil2011lei12527, brasil2017lei13460, brasil2021lei14129}. The National Digital Government Strategy for 2024--2027, instituted by Decree No. 12,069/2024 and revised in 2026, reinforces coordination among federative entities, inclusion, and the improvement of digital public services \cite{brasil2024decreto12069, brasil2026portaria5395}.

These legal frameworks make it possible to derive verifiable questions about municipal portals and channels: Is there an electronic means to request information? Can citizens file complaints and other submissions? Are deadlines and responsible parties identified? Can services be completed digitally? Verification must distinguish the publication of a link from the actual functionality of the service. Likewise, information that is missing, inaccessible, or hosted on external systems should not be automatically interpreted as the nonexistence of the service unless the protocol establishes a prior rule for such cases.

Digital accessibility is another relevant dimension. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 provide verifiable criteria for web content, whereas the Electronic Government Accessibility Model (\textit{Modelo de Acessibilidade em Governo Eletrônico}, eMAG) adapts accessibility recommendations to the Brazilian context \cite{w3c2023wcag22, brasil2014emag}. Automated auditing can identify some of the technical barriers but does not replace all manual checks, especially those related to navigation and content comprehension. In turn, public reviews or social media comments may provide exploratory signals of satisfaction, but they do not constitute a representative sample of municipal service users.

\section{Related Work}

This section positions the proposed framework with respect to three groups of studies: extensions of e-government maturity models, assessment instruments applied to local governments, and Brazilian instruments for monitoring digital government and transparency. Table~\ref{tab:related-work} summarizes this comparison.

The stage models presented in the previous section were subsequently extended. \cite{andersen2006egovernment} propose the Public Sector Process Rebuilding (PPR) model, which shifts the focus from technological capability to the core activities of government and to end users, a perspective that underpins this work's emphasis on what citizens can actually accomplish through digital channels. 

At the municipal scale, \cite{moon2002evolution}, drawing on a survey of local governments in the United States, found that the adoption of municipal websites was at an early stage and had not yet delivered the outcomes promised by the e-government rhetoric, which anticipates the distinction adopted here between digital presence and maturity. Recent reviews highlight the fragmentation of the field: \cite{david2023understanding} identify factors of municipal digital adoption related to people, processes, and technology, and \cite{vieira2026modelos} point to the diversity of maturity models in the Brazilian public sector and the absence of a consolidated instrument.

Among the instruments aimed at local governments, the LOSI, discussed in the previous section, assesses the portal of the most populous city in each country \cite{un2024egovernment}. With a similar scope, the \textit{Digital Governance in Municipalities Worldwide} survey, conducted since 2003 by the E-Governance Institute at Rutgers University, assesses the websites of large municipalities in five categories: privacy and security, usability, content, services, and citizen participation \cite{holzer2016digital}. Based on these data, \cite{manoharan2017global} group cities into profiles ranging from digitally marginal to digitally mature. These instruments are observational and multidimensional, but they focus on the largest cities in each country and do not incorporate the legal obligations of the Brazilian context. 

Within national settings, \cite{toleikiene2025assessing} assess the digital governance maturity of all 60 Lithuanian municipalities through a questionnaire and structural equation modeling, articulating strategy, public servants, and processes, whereas \cite{mkhitaryan2026digital} build, from publicly available data, a composite index for eleven Armenian municipalities across four dimensions: digital service availability, administrative efficiency, transparency, and participation. These studies show that multidimensional municipal assessment already has precedents.

In Brazil, several instruments monitor aspects of digital government, with different scopes and data sources. The ICT Electronic Government survey (\textit{TIC Governo Eletrônico}), conducted by Cetic.br, investigates ICT infrastructure and online service provision in city governments across the country through interviews with public managers \cite{cgi2024ticgov}. The Digital Government Map (\textit{Mapa de Governo Digital}), from the Digital Government Secretariat, and the governance surveys of the Federal Court of Accounts (TCU), in which the iGovTI was computed and which were replaced in 2024 by the iESGo, also rely on self-assessment questionnaires, with the TCU surveys restricted to federal organizations \cite{sgd2022mapa, tcu2024iesgo}. In the field of transparency, the Brazil Transparency Scale (\textit{Escala Brasil Transparente}, EBT) -- 360$^{\circ}$ Assessment, by the Office of the Comptroller General (CGU), combined the verification of active transparency with access-to-information requests, but its most recent edition, in 2020, covered only municipalities with more than 50,000 inhabitants, which excludes most municipalities in Mato Grosso \cite{cgu2020ebt}. 

The National Public Transparency Program (\textit{Programa Nacional de Transparência Pública}, PNTP), coordinated by the Association of Members of the Brazilian Courts of Accounts (Atricon) and carried out by the courts of accounts since 2022, assesses transparency portals against more than one hundred criteria, combining self-assessment by the audited bodies with validation by the courts \cite{atricon2026pntp}. These instruments are the closest references to the proposed framework, but they either focus on a single domain, such as transparency, or depend on self-reports by the assessed organizations. In a previous study, \cite{zahn2024mapping} mapped the communication channels of the general ombudsman offices of Brazilian state capitals, demonstrating the feasibility of direct observation of digital channels as a source of evidence.

These instruments already cover components relevant to the present proposal. The intended distinction, therefore, does not stem from introducing accessibility or interoperability in isolation, but from bringing together features that the reviewed instruments do not combine simultaneously, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:related-work}: census-based application to all municipalities of a state, regardless of size; multiple dimensions grounded in Brazilian legislation; data collection based predominantly on direct observation rather than on self-reports by the assessed organizations; and an evidence-recording protocol that enables the auditing and reapplication of the assessment.

\section{The Evaluation Framework}

The idea that governments progress through stages of digital maturity is not new: since the seminal work of~\cite{nolan1973} on stages of growth in Organizational Information Technology, and its adaptation by~\cite{layneLee2001} specifically to electronic government -- which describes the trajectory of a public portal as a movement from information cataloging to transaction, and from transaction to integration --, the Information Systems literature has treated maturity as something that unfolds in recognizable stages, rather than as an attribute that a municipality simply possesses or does not possess.

The problem is that this theoretical tradition, when operationalized in national indexes such as the United Nations E-Government Survey, was designed to compare countries, and when operationalized in Brazilian sectoral instruments such as the iGovTI of the Federal Court of Accounts (TCU), it was designed to assess institutional IT governance -- neither of them aims to capture what effectively occurs in the everyday relationship between a municipal government and the citizens it serves. It is precisely within this gap that the proposed framework is positioned: not as another generic maturity index, but as a translation of this theoretical tradition to the municipal scale.

This translation, however, is not an exercise without methodological grounding -- it follows the Design Science Research paradigm described by~\cite{marchSmith1995} and~\cite{hevner2004}, in which the object of knowledge is not a theory to be tested, but an artifact to be constructed: a construct (the ten dimensions), a model (the index aggregation structure), and a method (the data collection protocol). More specifically, the construction process of this artifact follows the six activities proposed by~\cite{peffers2007} -- problem identification, definition of objectives, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication, with this section being dedicated precisely to the design and development stage.

The recognition that this translation to the municipal scale must address a difficulty that classical stage models do not face (municipal digital maturity is not a single line of progress) lies in the coexistence of several fronts that advance at different speeds within the same government. This leads to the first structural decision of the framework: to treat maturity not as a single variable to be observed, but as a formative construct, according to the formulation of~\cite{diamantopoulosWinklhofer2001}, in which the dimensions compose the concept rather than being manifestations of a common latent cause.

This matters because a municipality may have an exemplary transparency portal and, at the same time, no effective mechanism for citizen participation, without this constituting an inconsistency in the index. It is therefore evidence itself that we are dealing with a multifaceted phenomenon, exactly the behavior predicted by the literature on formative constructs.

\subsection{Where Maturity Reveals Itself}

If maturity is multidimensional, it is necessary to understand which dimensions reveal it, in what order they emerge, and how this maturity can be captured. The response adopted by the framework follows a logic of concentric layers, each one presupposing the previous one. The elementary layer is the very existence of a digital communication channel, a website that works, responds, is secure, and is up to date~\cite{zahn2024mapping}. Without this minimum condition, no other dimension can even be evaluated; therefore, Digital Presence functions in the framework as a kind of entry point, rather than as a maturity differentiator in itself.

Once this entry point is ensured, it becomes necessary to shift the understanding from whether the government is digitally present to what this channel allows citizens to do. The first institutionalized response to this question in the Brazilian legal framework is the Ombudsman's Office: Law No. 13.460/2017 leaves no room for treating the existence of a channel for citizen submissions as an optional good practice. The same reasoning of normative obligation, but concerning information rather than citizen submissions, leads to Transparency, grounded in the Access to Information Law and in the already tested methodology of the Brazil Transparency Scale of the Office of the Comptroller General (CGU). Between these two dimensions lies Organizational Structure: without this layer, institutional communication lacks traceability.

Each of these four dimensions, when operationalized, was decomposed into binary or ordinal indicators rather than continuous subjective scales, following the principle that subjective scales introduce variance among evaluators that is not variance in the measured phenomenon, but noise from the instrument itself, the classical problem of inter-rater reliability discussed by~\cite{devellis2016} in the tradition of scale development. A binary indicator is verifiable by any evaluator with the same result -- a prerequisite for both scientific reproducibility and automation.

At this point, the framework shifts from ''what the government provides'' to ''what the citizen can do with it'', which justifies the introduction of Communication Channels and Participation as distinct dimensions. Participation, in particular, is treated here not as a binary category, but as a continuum that goes from information to consultation and from consultation to decision — a gradation borrowed directly from the United Nations E-Participation Index, which avoids the common mistake of equating a simple suggestion box with digital participatory budgeting. The final dimension of this first group, Service Digitalization, explicitly returns to the stage logic of Layne and Lee (2001) — cataloging, transaction, vertical integration, and horizontal integration.

\textbf{Why Go Beyond What Existing Instruments Already Cover}

Up to this point, the framework could be described as a careful synthesis of already established instruments. However, a synthesis that merely reorganizes what already exists does not, by itself, constitute a new contribution. It is at this point that the three dimensions that distinguish this framework from its predecessors are introduced, and each one responds to a question that the reviewed instruments simply do not ask.

The first missing question is: is the website usable by everyone? Digital Accessibility, assessed according to WCAG and its Brazilian adaptation, eMAG, fills this gap as a technical criterion auditable by an automated tool. The second missing question is: is the citizen actually satisfied? The approach adopted by the framework — treating public Google reviews and social media comments as a proxy for satisfaction through natural language processing — is an experimental approximation and is treated as such. The third missing question is: do the systems communicate with each other? It is this fragmentation that the Interoperability, Open Data, and Security dimension seeks to capture, drawing on the concept of ''core government systems´´ from the World Bank's GovTech Maturity Index.

Assigning a distinct data collection method to each dimension — manual checklist for some, automated scraping for others, natural language processing for satisfaction — applies the principle of methodological triangulation \cite{denzin1978}: using multiple methods so that the specific bias of one method (for example, the social desirability bias present in self-reported questionnaires, such as those used by iGovTI, INMSD, and the Digital Government Map) is mitigated by the presence of other methods that do not share the same limitation.

\textbf{From the Sum of the Parts to a Single Interpretation}

Once these ten dimensions are brought together, the framework faces its final design decision: how to transform ten distinct measures into something that a public manager, without training in research methodology, can interpret at a glance. This practical need justifies aggregating the dimensions into a single Municipal Digital Maturity Index through a weighted sum. This choice follows what the OECD's Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators (2008) describes as a compensatory aggregation model — as opposed to non-compensatory models, such as outranking models, which penalize very low performance in any isolated dimension even when overall averages are high —, and this choice carries a limitation that is stated here with academic transparency: the compensatory model implicitly assumes that a deficit in Participation may be compensated for by an excess of Digital Presence, which is normatively debatable.

The weights assigned to each dimension at this stage are therefore treated as a hypothesis rather than as a result. Assigning weights based on researchers' judgment without a structured process is a common weakness in amateur composite indexes; therefore, the framework commits to validating these weights through the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) \cite{saaty1980}, which formalizes pairwise comparison of criteria by experts with an auditable consistency ratio, or through the Delphi method \cite{dalkeyHelmer1963}, which formalizes the convergence of expert judgment through anonymous iterative rounds — two consolidated methods with more than sixty years of use in the multicriteria decision-making literature, which will be applied after the pilot, when real data are already available to guide the weighting process.

The choice of five maturity ranges, rather than a continuous scale expressed in points, derives from the same tradition of stage models that introduced this section: just as Layne and Lee (2001) described the maturation of a portal in named and recognizable stages, and just as the Capability Maturity Model (CMM) consolidated this logic of named stages for software process maturity \cite{paulk1993} — a tradition that precedes and directly influences maturity models in electronic government —, the IMDM provides the reader not with a number, but with a stage: something that can be compared across municipalities and monitored over time within the same municipality as the observatory is reapplied.

\subsection{Operationalization and Aggregation of the IMDM: Proposed Protocol}

To make the framework reproducible, the observational unit is the municipality, considering the official municipal government website, linked portals, officially indicated third-party services, and publicly available normative documents. Each piece of evidence must record the municipality, indicator, URL, date and time of access, source, assigned category, justification, and evaluator identification. In the case of external systems, the official association with the municipality must be documented. The mere existence of a link should not be confused with the functionality of the service.

The ten dimensions remain the conceptual scope of the framework. The pilot reported here included 31 manual questions from dimensions D1 to D7 and D10; D8 and D9 do not yet have collected data. Table 2 summarizes the operational status of the dimensions, without presenting planned components as if they had already been validated.

The calculation of the complete index is presented as a \textit{preliminary specification to be validated}, rather than as a result of the pilot. Each verifiable binary response may receive 1 (compliance) or 0 (non-compliance). For D1.5, the proposed coding is 1 (up to 30 days), 2/3 (30--180 days), 1/3 (more than 180 days), and 0 (no date), while noting that the updating of news content alone does not necessarily correspond to the updating of services. For D3.4, the proposed coding is 1 (consistent), 0.5 (partially consistent), and 0 (not consistent). For D7.1, the planned normalization is the observed stage divided by 4. The coding of D8 and D9 remains pending the definition and validation of their respective instruments.

Let $x_{m,d,j}\in[0,1]$ be the verifiable score of indicator $j$ of dimension $d$ in municipality $m$. The dimensional score and the index are provisionally defined as:
\begin{equation}
 S_{m,d}=\frac{\sum_{j\in V_{m,d}}x_{m,d,j}}{|V_{m,d}|},\qquad
 \mathrm{IMDM}_{m}=100\sum_{d=1}^{10}w_d S_{m,d},\qquad \sum_{d=1}^{10} w_d=1.
\end{equation}

Here, $V_{m,d}$ corresponds to the effectively verifiable indicators of a dimension. The absence of evidence, a temporarily inaccessible link, and the confirmed absence of a service must be recorded separately: responses marked as ``not possible to verify'' do not automatically receive a score of zero. The report must disclose the coverage $|V_{m,d}|/N_d$ of each dimension; the minimum completeness rule for comparing municipalities will be established before full-scale application. The IMDM is not calculated when D8 or D9 have not been measured, in order to avoid presenting a partial index as complete.

In the first future simulation, equal weights ($w_d=0.10$) may serve as a baseline, without constituting validated weights. AHP or Delphi, involving experts with defined profiles, may support an alternative weighting scheme; sensitivity analyses should compare equal weights, expert weights, and scenarios that reduce compensation across dimensions. To test the computational implementation, five \emph{provisional} ranges are defined: Initial ($0\leq\mathrm{IMDM}<20$), Basic ($20\leq\mathrm{IMDM}<40$), Intermediate ($40\leq\mathrm{IMDM}<60$), Advanced ($60\leq\mathrm{IMDM}<80$), and Integrated ($80\leq\mathrm{IMDM}\leq100$). The equal intervals constitute an operational convention for future implementation, not empirically validated cutoffs. Before assigning levels to municipalities, they must be evaluated by experts and through sensitivity analyses; this article does not calculate or present municipal maturity levels.

D1 functions as a stage for locating digital evidence, without automatically invalidating other dimensions if the main page is inaccessible and the municipality maintains linked official portals. D10 also requires caution: a privacy policy does not prove full compliance with the LGPD, and single sign-on does not demonstrate data integration. After the pilot, D10.3 (chatbot) may be analyzed together with D5, and D10.4 should be differentiated from the integration between systems described in Stage 4 of D7.1. These decisions will be documented before the census data collection.

For D8, it is proposed that equivalent pages from each municipality be selected in advance, such as the homepage, Ombudsman's Office, e-SIC, and a transactional service when available. The evaluation will combine automated verification of applicable WCAG 2.2 and eMAG criteria with manual inspection of keyboard navigation, form labeling, and content comprehension. For D9, service data or a questionnaire with users, when accessible, will constitute the primary measure; public comments may be used only as an exploratory signal, with documentation of selection bias and unequal availability across municipalities. As initial candidates for operationalization, D8 may include checks of keyboard navigation, identification of labels in forms, and barriers detected by an accessibility tool on predefined pages; D9 may record the existence of recent user surveys and the proportion of satisfaction only when the method, period, sample size, and denominator are documented. These candidate indicators require their own manual and pilot test. There are no results for these two dimensions at the current stage.
  
\section{Results and Discussion}

Considering the objective of conducting a preliminary assessment of the clarity of the manual indicators, a questionnaire containing closed-ended questions corresponding to the framework indicators and open-ended questions intended to identify interpretation difficulties was applied. The questionnaire was sent via \textit{Google Forms} to 10 individuals, including students and researchers from the Computer Engineering program, who carried out a guided inspection of the portals in September 2026. Participants were asked to answer the closed-ended questions based on the information available on the portals of the selected municipalities, thereby simulating the application of the framework. Agreement among participants is taken as evidence of indicator clarity: if an indicator is sufficiently clear, different evaluators are expected to interpret and apply it in a similar manner. Therefore, the dispersion of the responses was used to verify the degree of agreement regarding the interpretation and application of each indicator.

With the objective of reducing the influence of specific characteristics of municipal portals on the results, three municipalities of different sizes (Cuiabá-MT, Sinop-MT, and Vera-MT) were selected as targets for the framework simulation. Among the participants, all 10 answered the form referring to the municipality of Vera-MT, while only 6 answered the form for the municipality of Cuiabá-MT, and only 8 answered the form for the municipality of Sinop-MT.

No probabilistic sampling technique was employed; the participants were selected by convenience, based on availability and accessibility. Thus, the results obtained are not intended to statistically represent a specific population, but rather to provide preliminary evidence regarding the clarity and applicability of the indicators.

To analyze the distribution of responses to the indicators, the Variation Ratio was adopted, considering the categorical nature (``Yes'' and ``No'', for example) of the variables and the interest in identifying the concentration or dispersion of the participants' responses. Thus, the Variation Ratio, referred to as the Dispersion Rate (DR) in this work, was used to quantify disagreement among categorical responses, both nominal and ordinal. For ordinal variables, the measure does not use the ordering information of the categories. The measure considers the proportion of responses that do not correspond to the modal category, allowing the degree of agreement/disagreement among the evaluators to be assessed \cite{freeman1965}. Since the maximum attainable DR increases with the number of response categories, comparisons between binary items and items with more categories (D1.1, D1.5, D2.5, D3.4, and D7.1) should be interpreted with caution.

Based on the DRs calculated individually for each municipality, the mean DR was calculated for each indicator, representing the average level of dispersion across the municipal contexts. Additionally, the standard deviation of the municipalities' DRs (SD-DR), computed as a population standard deviation over the three municipalities, makes it possible to distinguish a divergence that occurs similarly across municipalities from a divergence concentrated in a specific municipality. For the purpose of interpreting the results, operational ranges were established for the mean DR. Since the observed values ranged between 0\% and 50\%, this interval was divided into three ranges of approximately equal amplitude: mean DR values up to 16\% were classified as high clarity, values greater than 16\% and up to 33\% as medium clarity, and values greater than 33\% as low clarity. Similarly, considering that the highest observed SD-DR was 15.9 percentage points (p.p.), the variability among municipalities was classified as low (up to 5 p.p.), medium (greater than 5 and up to 10 p.p.), or high (greater than 10 p.p.). Both classifications were defined specifically for the analysis of this study and do not constitute universal statistical cutoffs.

The joint analysis of the two measures distinguishes three situations. A high mean DR combined with a low SD-DR indicates that dispersion is high and occurs similarly across municipalities, which provides stronger evidence that the divergence is related to the indicator itself. A low mean DR combined with a low SD-DR indicates responses that are predominantly in agreement and consistent across municipalities. A high SD-DR indicates a relevant difference among municipalities, suggesting that factors specific to a given portal may be influencing the dispersion, although the divergence cannot be attributed exclusively to the indicator or to the municipalities. As a second source of evidence, the responses to the open-ended questions were used to contextualize the observed divergences, helping to identify whether they stem from understanding the indicator or from locating and interpreting the information required to apply it.

Of the 31 indicators, 11 showed high clarity, 10 showed medium clarity, and 10 showed low clarity. High clarity is concentrated in items that verify the existence of a directly observable element, such as the official website and institutional domain (D1.1 and D1.2), the link to and functional channel of the Ombudsman's Office (D2.1 and D2.2), and the Transparency Portal (D4.1), all with zero dispersion in the three municipalities. In contrast, the low-clarity indicators (D2.3, D2.4, D2.5, D3.1, D3.4, D4.5, D4.8, D6.3, D7.1, and D10.4) generally require evaluators to search beyond the main pages, to compare documents, or to apply distinctions that the question does not make explicit.

The joint analysis reinforces this interpretation. D3.4, which asks whether the administrative structure presented on the website is consistent with the legislation, reached the highest mean DR (50.00\%) with no variation across municipalities (SD-DR of 0.0 p.p.), the pattern most strongly associated with a problem in the indicator itself; it was also reported as not objectively determinable. The qualitative responses help explain several other cases (Table~\ref{tab:qualitative-responses}). For D2.3, evaluators in the three municipalities stated that they did not know who the ombudsman was, which suggests that the question does not specify where and in what form the name should appear. For D2.4, an evaluator could not determine whether the deadline established by the Access to Information Law also applied to Ombudsman's Office submissions, and D7.1 was reported as not understood. The responses also pointed to overlaps between indicators: D4.6 and D4.8 with D2.2 and D2.4 when the information service is operated by the Ombudsman's Office, and D10.4 with Stage~4 of D7.1, both of which assess single sign-on.

Indicators with high SD-DR, such as D3.1 (15.9 p.p.) and D1.5 (15.3 p.p.), show divergence concentrated in a specific municipality, in both cases Cuiabá, where the DR reached 50.00\%. In such cases, the difficulty may be related more to the organization of each portal than to the wording of the indicator, as also suggested by an evaluator's report of difficulty locating the e-SIC on the Cuiabá website. Taken together, these results do not validate the instrument, but they identify concrete targets for its refinement: providing operational definitions and examples for items that require judgment, clarifying the legal distinctions involved in deadline-related items, restructuring D7.1, and eliminating the overlaps reported between indicators.

\section{Final Considerations}

This article presented the design of a multidimensional framework for assessing municipal digital maturity and a preliminary proposal for operationalizing the Municipal Digital Maturity Index (IMDM). The instrument brings together ten dimensions, seeking to integrate institutional aspects, transparency, services, participation, accessibility, and interaction with citizens. Its intended contribution to Information Systems is to combine observable criteria, evidence recording, and common data collection rules, enabling future comparison and reapplication across the municipalities of Mato Grosso.

The initial demonstration was limited to assessing the clarity of 31 manual indicators in three municipalities of different sizes: Cuiabá, Sinop, and Vera. The dispersion rates and the evaluators’ comments identified questions with more stable interpretation as well as specific problems related to wording, evidence location, and possible overlap among criteria. These findings support the refinement of the instrument, but they do not demonstrate its full validity nor allow inferences about the maturity of the municipalities studied.

The main limitations are the convenience sampling of the evaluators, the lack of complete information about the number and training of the participants, the absence of a formal measure of inter-rater reliability, and the non-application of the Digital Accessibility and Citizen Satisfaction dimensions. The weights, the final rules for unverifiable data, and the thresholds of the five maturity levels also remain subject to validation. The weighted-sum model may mask very low performance in specific dimensions; therefore, the index should be reported together with the dimensional scores and the evidence coverage rates.

An additional limitation, directly related to Goodhart’s Law \cite{goodhart1984problems}, is that the indicators applied in this first survey assess the declared existence of a document or channel, but not the integrity of its content. An attached expenditure file that is empty or an open data portal without records would be registered as satisfying the indicator, creating room for municipal teams to improve or increase the IMDM score without a corresponding change in the actual transparency offered to citizens.

As future work, it is planned to revise the ambiguous questions, document a coding manual, repeat the pilot with independent evaluators, operationalize D8 and D9, submit the weighting proposal to expert evaluation, conduct sensitivity analyses, and incorporate content verification (and not only presence verification) into the indicators related to attached files and/or documents, especially in D3, D4, and D10. Only after these steps will the planned census application to the 142 municipalities be carried out, accompanied by a spatial visualization and an evidence repository that support the reproduction of the results and longitudinal monitoring. This agenda preserves the artifact development and evaluation character typical of Design Science Research, without anticipating results that have not yet been obtained.
